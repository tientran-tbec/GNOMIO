Corporate Social Responsibility — A New Concept of "Market"
Maybe Ben & Jerry's and The Body Shop set themselves up for a fall by appearing to have a monopoly on making an honest buck. In an age of increased scrutiny of business practices, both faced serious questions about whether their business behaviour matched their ethical rhetoric. But both companies helped pioneer the modern notion of corporate social responsibility, an idea that has now swept through the business world.

Ben and Jerry's

ABen & Jerry's offers a very sweet benefits package to employees. First, every one of the 700+ Ben & Jerry's workers is eligible for three pints of ice cream a day. That's a small part of a larger benefits package that the company claims has a dollar value three to five times greater than average for the food industry. But what sets Ben & Jerry's apart from many companies is a deep concern for how employees treat other employees, a concern that can be seen in its written values statement and in the benefits it provides.
BWorkers are further entitled to paid family leave and may take advantage of the Employee Stock Purchase Program to purchase company stock at a 15% discount. Child Care Resource and Referral is available to all employees to help them find local day-care and other child care services. There is also a Joy Committee at Ben & Jerry's. Its mission, using a portion of the company's profits, is to infuse joy into the day-to-day work life of Ben & Jerry's employees. There's also a company-wide profit-sharing plan.

The Body Shop

CHistory of The Body Shop: Anita Roddick started The Body Shop with a mere £4,000 and a dream. With over 1,900 stores in 50 countries, Roddick has built a major success story. Key to her success has been her insistence on developing products, such as skin and hair care, that are made only from natural ingredients and were never tested on animals. Her products appeal to the conscientious consumer concerned about using natural ingredients and avoiding animal testing. Through The Body Shop Foundation she has also donated millions to communities and charities in developing countries, as well as to environmental and human rights campaigns.
DPart of the secret of The Body Shop's early success was that it had created a market niche for itself. The company was not competing in the traditional cosmetics market — selling glamour and sex appeal. Rather, it was selling natural beauty products aimed at the socially conscious consumer. The products were distinctive in the marketplace and built customer loyalty for the brand. The Body Shop's ability to target that consumer effectively helped it avoid large amounts spent on advertising, allowing the company to pass those savings on to the consumer.
EBy the mid-1990s, however, The Body Shop faced growing competition, forcing it to begin its first major advertising initiative and revamp its product line. The company added more than 300 new products to replace old ones. Now the challenge to The Body Shop is how to maintain its customer base when its unique selling proposition — "natural beauty products" — has been successfully copied by the competitors, and when the newer, fresher brands that have emerged challenge the company's image. Roddick claims The Body Shop's failure to develop its brand in recent years has been one of its key mistakes.
FThe Competition: The Body Shop lost market share in the late 1990s to product-savvy competitors that offered similar cosmetics at lower prices. For example, Bath & Body Works has been particularly successful at targeting a similar market segment with a series of cheaper products that appeal to a similar customer. As a result, The Body Shop has struggled to maintain its prices, quality, and customer base in the face of competition from companies with the advantage of size and resources.
GThe Problem: The Body Shop has used safe and timid advertising over the last decade, decreasing market share and brand value. Roddick herself admits that The Body Shop has failed to keep the brand aspirational. The company needs to reconnect with younger consumers and re-establish its values and ethics platform in a fresh, relevant way. The company had a strong first-mover advantage in the "natural cosmetics" niche, but it failed to sustain and grow its brand position as the market expanded and competition increased.

Psychology of New Product Adoption
AIn today's hypercompetitive marketplace, companies that successfully introduce new products are more likely to flourish than those that don't. Businesses spend billions of dollars making better "mousetraps" only to find consumers roundly rejecting them. Studies show that new products fail at the stunning rate of between 40% and 90%, depending on the category, and the odds haven't changed much in the past 25 years. In the U.S. packaged goods industry, for instance, companies introduce 30,000 products every year, but 70% to 90% of them don't stay on store shelves for more than 12 months. Most innovative products — those that create new product categories or revolutionize old ones — are also unsuccessful. According to one study, 47% of first movers have failed, meaning that approximately half the companies that pioneered new product categories later pulled out of those businesses.
BAfter the fact, experts and novices alike tend to dismiss unsuccessful innovations as bad ideas that were destined to fail. Companies that failed to sell pet food online or that unsuccessfully tried to sell groceries through the internet were not simply ahead of their time, the argument goes; their businesses were fundamentally flawed from the start. But this kind of hindsight bias blinds us to the fact that many innovations are sound ideas whose failure is not inevitable but rather stems from a failure to understand and overcome the psychological obstacles to adoption.
CNew products often require consumers to change their behavior. As companies know, those behavior changes entail costs. Consider a drug company that is launching a new pill that must be taken three times a day instead of once. Switching to the new drug might lead to better outcomes for patients, yet many won't do it because of the psychological cost of changing their routine. Or consider a new product from a computer company that is far superior in usability. The new product may sit on shelves because people do not want to replace their current computer that functions well and are familiar with.
DWhat businesses don't take into account, however, are the psychological costs associated with behavior change. Many products fail not because they are bad products, but because companies mistakenly focus only on the direct or economic costs and benefits of switching to a new product. While a company may appropriately and effectively reduce the economic costs of switching by offering cash rebates, discounts, or other financial incentives, it may fail to account for and address the customer's psychological costs.
ECompanies have long assumed that people will adopt new products that deliver more value or utility than existing ones. This assumption underlies what Everett Rogers, the sociologist who popularised the concept of the "tipping point," called the "better mousetrap" fallacy — the mistaken belief that building a better product is a sufficient condition for its success. In other words, conventional wisdom says that if a new product is truly better, consumers will buy it. But this belief is wrong for many products.
FIn 2002, psychologist Daniel Kahneman won the Nobel Prize in economics for a body of work that explores why and when individuals make irrational decisions. Most relevant here is Kahneman and Tversky's work on "prospect theory," which holds that people are more sensitive to potential losses than to potential gains of the same size. This finding suggests an inherent psychological advantage for existing products over newer ones, because people weight potential losses heavily when they contemplate giving up a valued possession.
GFirst, people evaluate the attractiveness of an alternative based not on its objective, or actual, value but on its subjective value — the perceived gains and losses associated with the alternative. In a series of studies, Kahneman and Tversky demonstrated that people evaluate attractiveness in terms of gains and losses from a current reference point, rather than in terms of final states of wealth or welfare.
HLoss aversion leads people to value products that they already possess — those that are part of their endowment — more than the same products when they don't own them. This is the "endowment effect." In one classic experiment, some participants were given a coffee mug and then asked how much they'd be willing to accept to sell the mug. Others, who had not been given mugs, were asked how much they would be willing to pay to acquire one. The sellers wanted about twice as much as the buyers were willing to pay.
IIn a 1990 paper, Thaler and his colleagues describe a series of experiments they conducted to measure the magnitude of the endowment effect. One experiment involved Cornell University students who were randomly assigned to be "sellers," "buyers," or "choosers." Sellers were given a coffee mug and were asked to indicate the price at which they would be willing to sell the mug. Buyers indicated the price they were willing to pay. Choosers indicated whether they preferred the mug or a sum of money in various amounts. The median selling price was about twice the median buying price — and about twice the median amount the choosers said they preferred over the mug.
JKahneman and Tversky's research also explains why people tend to stick with what they have even if a better alternative exists. This phenomenon — what they called "status quo bias" — describes the tendency of people to want things to remain as they are or to choose a default option. In one famous experiment, they gave some students a coffee mug and others a chocolate bar and then gave each student the option to trade. Only 10% actually chose to make the trade.
KInterestingly, most people seem oblivious to the existence of the behaviors implicit in the endowment effect and the status quo bias. In surveys, most people claim that they are willing to swap for something of equal value, but their actual behavior suggests the opposite. This discrepancy suggests companies could help people understand their own psychological biases, thereby making it easier for them to consider new products with an unbiased mind.

All the Ways Women Are Still Pressured to Put Family Before Career
AThere's no denying that women around the world have made great strides toward equality in the past century. One hundred years ago, women in the U.S. didn't have the right to vote. Now, more women than men have bachelor's degrees, and women make up nearly half the national workforce. Yet even as women have gained the right to work outside the home, the implicit societal message is that we still expect women to put family over career, says feminist psychotherapist Silvia Dutchevici.
BBut despite all this newfound opportunity, the prevailing societal attitudes about what women are historically supposed to do — be a wife, mother, and homemaker — haven't evolved at nearly the same rate. "We've added to women's roles — yes, you can work — but we haven't taken away the old expectations," Dutchevici says. "So now we expect women to do everything. We expect them to prioritize their family over their own needs, and we also expect them to work."
C"Most women try to balance work and family," Dutchevici says, "but that balance is seldom equal." In fact, she says women essentially have two jobs: their career and the household work they do at home. "Working women are generally still responsible for more housework and childcare than their working partners," she says. "That's really a double burden." The concept of the "second shift" — doing domestic work after a full day of paid work — is still a modern reality for many women.
D"No matter how successful she is, the burden of running a household still falls on the woman's shoulders," Lashchyk says. And even more subtly, a woman's very sense of identity might be wrapped up in her role as wife and mother. "Women are often raised to be people-pleasers, conditioned to care for others at the expense of their own needs and ambitions," she says. "That cultural conditioning is very hard to override."
E"In many people's minds, a woman's career success pales in comparison to having a family," she says. "Especially if that woman is not married or doesn't have children, she might be met with questions like, 'How do you have time for this? When are you going to settle down?' Whereas a man who was equally successful professionally and single would rarely be met with those questions." This double standard affects the way women see themselves and their choices.
F"Unfortunately, many workplace policies regarding taking time off to care for family do not reflect the changing times," Dutchevici says. "While the U.S. has made progress with maternity leave, paternity leave is still minimal, meaning that by default it often falls to women to take time off after the birth of a child." Research shows that companies with extended paternity leave find that men take on a larger share of childcare, ultimately benefiting women's ability to focus on their career.
GAnother less visible way the modern workplace forces women to choose family over career has to do with the fact that women are often penalized professionally for pushing back pregnancy. "Women are expected to have children, and if they don't by their early 30s, they can feel pressure to pause their careers," says Jeni Mayorskaya, CEO of Stork Club, a family-planning benefits company. "But if they do have children young, their career often suffers." It's a catch-22 that men simply don't face in the same way.
HSo what can women do to combat these societal pressures? Finding workplaces that offer flexible schedules, work-at-home options, and strong family leave policies can help. Seeking out mentors and role models — both within and outside of work — and advocating for yourself in the workplace are also key. But above all, Lashchyk says it comes down to putting yourself first. "I tell women: treat yourself like your most valued client," she says. "Make time for your own health, your own development, and your own ambitions. That's not selfish — that's self-preservation."
